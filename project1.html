Background

The Prokudin-Gorskii photo collection are a series of photographs taken around 1907 using blue, green and red color filters. The idea being that the three negatives could be combined to produce a full color image. While there was no such technology at the time in the time of the modern computer we can use technology (and some math) to fulfill his vision!

Alignment

To align the images we need some way to score how similar our images are to each other. One such metric is the Normalized Cross-Correlation or NCC. If we think of our iamges as vectors we can compare any two vectors by the dot product. Which would be 0 for orthogonal vectors, |a||b| for vectors that are scalar multiples in the same direction, and negative in the opposite direction. While this works we can make this a bit cleaner by normalizing our vectors by centering their distribution around 0 by subtracting their means and normalizing them. This bounds the result of the dot product to -1, 1 and more accurately compares similar vectors that may be offset by a mean. $$\frac{(a-\bar{a})\cdot(b-\bar{b})}{{|a-\bar{a}|}{|b-\bar{b}|}}$$ From here we need to start searching for alignments. For a simple approach we can just serach across a small pixel range of offsets such as -12 to 12 pixels on the x and y axis using downscaled images. For this we use a small subsection of the image from the center and compare the red and blue channels to the green channel.

The results look great. However, trying a larger window on the full size images takes an incredibly long time. Getting a good result on a full size image took my computer about 10 minutes!

(Faster) Allignment

So how can we get faster?

Notably, the smaller scale images look pretty well aligned already, so why not use this? We can use these as guides for the larger scale images!

We start by downscaling the images and finding a good alignment at that level. This gives us a starting point for a more accurate alignment at a slightly less downscaled image.

After some trial and error I found 1/16th to be a good starting point. I also made it so the displacement range shrinks as we reach larger images. This reduces the run time and leads to minimal consequences as by the time we reach 1/4th scale, for example, it's likely we're only very slightly adjusting the image.

This brings us to the final results! I think they're pretty good.
