Background

From the outside view the idea of a panorama seems somewhat trivial as we have a device in our pockets that can so easily compute them. However, if upon thinking about how you would actually automate this process questions start to arise. For example, how do we actually make each individual image fit a single wider flat perspective? At that how do align features within each image, espeically with human imperfection?

Part A.2: Recover Homographies

First we need to understand how to relate two images that only differ by a rotation. Naturally a rotation will lead to images appearing to be along different flat planes. It can be shown that we can apply a project transform, a homography, to align them. But how do we calculate this homography? We can estimate the transform by using least squares. To apply least squares we need an equation in the format of \(Ax=b\). We need to find a way to format the input and output as \(A\) and \(b\). To setup the problem we first can explicitly write out the transformation assuming we have some homography matrix H and with source and destination points. $$\begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ \lambda \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} \frac{x'}{\lambda} \\ \frac{y'}{\lambda} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ To find A let's start by expanding the matrix multiplicaton. $$\lambda=xg+hy+1$$ $$\begin{align} x'&=\frac{xa+by+c}{\lambda} \\ x'&=\frac{xa+by+c}{xg+hy+1} \\ x'xg+x'yh+x'&=xa+by+c \\ x'&=xa+by+c-x'xg-x'yh \end{align}$$ $$\begin{align} y'&=\frac{xd+ye+f}{xg+hy+1} \\ y'xg+y'yh+y'&=xd+ye+f \\ y'&=xd+ye+f-y'xg-y'yh \end{align}$$ From here we can fix b as \(x'\) and \(y'\) and we can arrange the unknowns of \(H\) as \(x\) to make a reasonable \(A\) matrix. $$\begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x'x & -x'y \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -y'x & -y'y \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \\ e \\ f \\ g \\ h \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}$$ Finally we just apply least squares. Below is the correspondences for the first image and the calculated \(H\).

Part A.3: Warp the Images

Now we can apply the homography. To do this we want to apply an inverse warp to map output pixels to input pixels. This prevents holes from appearing in the output as naturally not every input pixel perfectly maps to an output. However, this also introduces another problem where the projection causes only a small portion of the image to be used in the final panorama. I solved this by simply doubling the width of the output canvas and translating the output pixel left by w (our transformation becomes \((T @ H)^{-1}\) where \(T\) is a rightward translation by the width, which upon inverse warping becomes a leftward translation).

Here is our warped image.
Notably extremely warped edges have visible jagged edges upon close inspection (look around the table on the left in the first image for a clear example). This happens due to the fact that we're only mapping the nearest pixel to the inverse transform of the output coordinate. In reality this mapping rarely lies perfectly on an integer, so it only makes sense that we're losing information when we just use the nearest pixel. We can solve this by using bilinear interpolation, which is where we use the weighted average of nearby pixels for the output pixel.

Part A.4: Blend the Images into a Mosaic

Finally we can begin blending. To do this I segmented the image into three parts: pixels belonging to the first image, both images, and the second.
Mask of shared pixels for each panorama
The first and third category are easy enough, just copy them over. The second category though is a bit more interesting. To make the transition smooth I apply a weighted average based on how far each pixel is to each image. If our mask were a rectangle, pixels along the left side of the shared area are completely from the first image, pixels in the middle are a 50/50 split, and images on the far right are completely from the second. But how do we actually do this. We can use the scipy function distance_transform_edt (similar to the bwdist function in matlab). This function given a matrix of 1s and 0s, finds the distance to the closest 0 for each 1. This gives us a clean way to find the proportion of each image as the following: $$ w1=\frac{d1}{d1+d2} $$ $$ w2=\frac{d2}{d1+d2} $$ Putting this all together below are the output images!
There is still some misalignment (I believe this may be due to human error when selecting correspondances, as when I made many more correspondances results usually improved), but visibly not too bad at all. It's also entirely possible its due to the distortion in the lens I was using but to my understanding the distortion shouldn't be too bad.
